  - q: What are subscription unit economics?
    a: >-
      The per-customer math that determines whether growth creates value or
      consumes it: what it costs to acquire a customer, how much contribution
      margin that customer generates per cycle, how long they stay, and
      therefore how many cycles it takes to earn back the acquisition cost. A
      business can grow revenue rapidly while every new cohort loses money, and
      it will look successful in every dashboard that reports revenue rather
      than contribution by cohort. The unit math is what makes that visible
      before the balance sheet does.
  - q: What is cohort payback period?
    a: >-
      The number of billing cycles it takes for a group of customers acquired in
      the same period to generate enough cumulative contribution margin to cover
      what it cost to acquire them. It is the single most useful number in a
      subscription business because it combines acquisition efficiency,
      retention, and margin into one figure, and because it directly controls
      how fast the business can grow without running out of cash. A shorter
      payback period means each dollar recycles faster, which is a growth
      constraint dressed as an accounting metric.
  - q: How should I calculate LTV for a subscription business?
    a: >-
      Carefully, and with contribution margin rather than revenue. The common
      shortcut, average revenue divided by churn rate, produces a number that is
      wrong in two directions at once: it uses revenue instead of margin, and it
      assumes a constant churn rate when real retention curves flatten over
      time, which understates the value of long-tenured customers and overstates
      the value of new ones. The defensible version builds from cohort retention
      curves and contribution margin per cycle, and reports LTV as a curve over
      time rather than a single lifetime number nobody can verify.
  - q: What is the relationship between churn and growth?
    a: >-
      Churn sets the size of the hole you refill before growth starts. At 5%
      monthly churn, you replace roughly half your base every year just to stand
      still, so the acquisition budget is doing maintenance work before it does
      any growing. This is why retention improvements compound and acquisition
      improvements do not: a better onboarding flow keeps paying on every future
      cohort, while a strong media quarter ends when the quarter does. It is
      also why a subscription business with a retention problem cannot buy its
      way out, only more expensively into the same place.
  - q: Is order frequency more important than churn?
    a: >-
      For non-subscription and flexible-cadence models, often yes, and it is
      badly under-managed. Two customers with identical retention and identical
      order value can differ by a factor of two in annual contribution purely on
      interval. Frequency also responds to work that retention messaging does
      not touch: replenishment timing, assortment breadth, and the default
      cadence you set at signup. The caution is that frequency bought with
      discounting is not frequency, it is a margin transfer with a nicer chart.
  - q: Why do cohorts get worse over time?
    a: >-
      Usually because acquisition scaled into a worse audience, and the
      retention program is taking the blame for a sourcing decision. When each
      successive cohort retains a little worse than the one before it while
      nothing in the lifecycle program has changed, the customers changed. That
      is a paid media and channel-mix finding, not a CRM one, and it is the most
      commonly misdiagnosed pattern in subscription growth. Reading retention by
      acquisition source rather than in aggregate is what exposes it.

## The model that looks fine right up until it does not

A subscription business can grow revenue for years while every cohort it
acquires loses money, and almost every dashboard in the company will describe
that as success. Revenue rises, subscriber count rises, monthly churn sits at a
number that sounds acceptable, and the thing that would have shown the problem,
contribution margin by acquisition cohort over time, is not on a dashboard
anywhere.

The unit math is not a finance exercise that happens after the marketing. It is
the thing that decides whether the marketing was worth doing.

## Cohort payback is the number that runs the business

Of all the metrics available, cohort payback period does the most work, because
it collapses acquisition cost, retention, and margin into one figure and because
it directly governs how fast the business can grow.

Payback is a cash constraint before it is a profitability one. A business whose
cohorts pay back in three cycles can recycle each acquisition dollar four times
a year. At nine cycles, the same dollar turns over less than half as often, and
growth becomes a financing question rather than a marketing one. Two businesses
with identical LTV to CAC ratios and different payback periods are not in the
same business.

This is also where retention improvements show their real value. A modest
improvement in early retention does not just add a few months of revenue at the
end of a customer's life. It pulls the payback curve left for every future
cohort, permanently, and that compounding is the honest argument for funding
retention work. Not that it is cheaper than acquisition, which invites lazy
conclusions, but that it keeps paying after the quarter ends.

## LTV, done in a way that survives scrutiny

The standard shortcut, average revenue divided by churn rate, is wrong twice.
It uses revenue where it should use contribution margin, and it assumes a
constant churn rate when actual retention curves decay steeply and then flatten.
The flattening matters: customers who survive the early window behave very
differently from the average, and a formula built on a single blended rate
cannot see them.

The version that holds up is built from the retention curve itself, cohort by
cohort, with contribution margin per cycle rather than top-line revenue, and
reported as a curve over time instead of a lifetime figure nobody can check. It
is less quotable and considerably more useful, particularly in the meeting where
someone wants to raise acquisition spend on the strength of an LTV number that
was calculated optimistically two years ago.

> Any LTV you cannot rebuild from a cohort table is a hypothesis with a decimal
> point on it.

## Frequency and breadth, the levers nobody staffs

Retention gets the attention because churn is dramatic. But in most catalogs the
larger variance in customer value comes from interval and assortment, not
survival. Two customers who both stay eighteen months can differ enormously in
contribution depending on how often they order and how many categories they buy
into.

Both respond to work that has nothing to do with retention messaging: the
default cadence set at signup, replenishment timing that matches actual
consumption rather than a marketing calendar, and cross-sell into adjacent needs
that raises breadth without training the customer to wait for a promotion. The
constraint throughout is margin. Frequency purchased with discounts is not a
frequency gain, it is a margin transfer, and it will show up in the cohort
contribution curve even while the order-count chart looks excellent.

## Involuntary churn is a unit-economics problem

It belongs in this conversation and not only in the churn one, because the
recovered subscriber is worth their full remaining contribution curve, at
essentially zero acquisition cost. That makes payment recovery the highest-return
line item in most subscription businesses and the one most likely to be owned by
nobody.

Dunning sequences, card-updater coverage, retry timing tuned to when balances
actually replenish, and pre-expiration outreach are unglamorous operational work
with a return that outperforms nearly any campaign competing for the same
attention.

## Reading retention by source, not in aggregate

When cohorts degrade steadily while the lifecycle program has not changed, the
lifecycle program is not the cause. Acquisition moved into a worse audience, and
the aggregate retention curve is averaging the good sources and the bad ones into
a single line that hides it.

Splitting retention by acquisition channel, campaign, and offer is what makes
this visible, and it usually reveals that a channel judged on cost per
acquisition has been buying customers who leave. That is a media allocation
finding, which is why this lane and the paid media lane cannot honestly be run
in separate rooms.

## How I work this lane

I build the cohort tables directly, because the interesting behavior is always
in the detail a blended average erases. Then payback, contribution by cohort,
frequency and breadth, and retention by source, in that order, because each one
constrains the next. The output is not a dashboard. It is a small number of
decisions: what a point of retention is worth, what payback period the business
can afford, and which acquisition sources should be paying more or less than
they currently are.

Amazon takes [more than half](https://www.marketplacepulse.com/articles/amazon-takes-a-50-cut-of-sellers-revenue) of a seller's revenue. Costco caps your markup at [14 percent](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/costco-reveals-keeps-prices-low-183300293.html). On a marketplace, your first sale to a customer is barely a sale. It is paid acquisition, and you already paid for it in margin. The mistake is treating that first order as the prize. The prize is the second order, and the second order should happen on your own site, where you keep the margin and you own the customer. The marketplace is how you meet them. Your site is how you keep them.

Most brands run these two channels as rivals, or worse, as one profit center and one afterthought. The team defends marketplace revenue because the volume is real, then burns a rising ad budget trying to acquire first-time buyers on the DTC site, the most expensive customer to win and the least likely to buy. That is backward. The marketplace is the cheapest high-intent entry point you will ever have. Your site is where the economics finally work. The job is to connect them.

## The marketplace sale is acquisition, not profit

Add up what the platform takes before you call a marketplace order a sale. Marketplace Pulse estimated Amazon's all-in take rate at more than 50 percent of a seller's revenue, up from about 40 percent five years earlier. The pieces: a 15 percent referral fee, 20 to 35 percent for Fulfillment by Amazon, and up to 15 percent on advertising to stay visible. Some sellers reported handing over 60 to 70 percent. Costco runs a different model and the result for your margin is similar: it caps markups near 14 percent on national brands and 15 percent on its own label, so you sell in at close to cost in exchange for volume and shelf credibility.

So the marketplace order is not where you make money. It is where a customer who already decided to buy finds you, on a platform that converts better than your site and costs you nothing in ad spend up front. That is worth a great deal. A cold prospect on your DTC site rarely buys on the first visit. A shopper on a marketplace product page has already cleared the hard part. You are not paying to create demand. You are paying a toll to capture demand that exists.

The catch is the one every brand underprices. On the marketplace you rent the customer. You do not get their email, you cannot build the relationship, and the platform can change the rules, raise the fees, or promote its own label against you whenever it wants. A marketplace-only brand is a brand with no customers, only transactions.

## The money is in the second order

Price the first order as acquisition and the whole strategy flips. The return on that acquisition is not the margin on order one. It is the lifetime that follows, and the lifetime is where the math turns.

Repeat customers behave nothing like first-timers. Across more than 12,000 merchants, Gorgias found repeat customers were [21 percent of the customer base but drove 44 percent of revenue](https://opensend.com/post/repeat-purchase-rate-ecommerce) and 46 percent of orders. And the odds of the next order climb with each one: the chance a buyer returns rises from [27 percent after the first order to 49 percent after the second and 62 percent after the third](https://blog.smile.io/repeat-customers-profitable/). A customer who has bought twice is far likelier to buy again than a cold one is to buy at all. This is the same reason [the second purchase is the real conversion](/blog/the-second-purchase-is-the-real-conversion/): the first order proves intent, the second proves a habit, and the habit is the asset.

Now put the two facts side by side. The marketplace gives you the first order cheaply but keeps the customer. Your site gives you the full margin and the data, but first-time acquisition there is expensive and uncertain. The strategy writes itself. Let the marketplace do what it is good at, winning the first order. Move everything after it to the channel where repeat is cheap, margin is whole, and the relationship is yours.

## The move: meet them on the marketplace, keep them on your site

This is not "quit Amazon." Quitting the best first-purchase channel to chase first purchases with paid ads is how brands light money on fire. The move is to run the marketplace as a funded acquisition line and your site as the retention and margin engine, with a deliberate path between them.

Decide which products lead and which products keep. Lead on the marketplace with the hero product, the one with the reviews and the search demand, the easy yes for a new buyer. Keep the consumables, refills, bundles and accessories as the reason to come back, and make those naturally better on your site. A customer who bought the device on Amazon should find the refills, the subscription and the expansion pack easier and more rewarding to buy from you.

## The low-friction bridge, inside the rules

Here is where most advice gets reckless. You cannot drop a "buy direct on our website" flyer in the Amazon box. Amazon's [Seller Code of Conduct](https://ecomengine.com/blog/amazon-product-inserts) is explicit: you may not attempt to circumvent the Amazon sales process or divert Amazon customers to another website. You also cannot ask for positive reviews or pay for them. Break those rules and you risk the account that is feeding you customers in the first place. The bridge has to be built from what is allowed, and plenty is.

Give them a real reason to tell you who they are. Product registration and warranty activation are permitted, service-oriented, and they capture an email legitimately. A two-year warranty that takes thirty seconds to activate on your site is a fair trade the customer wants to make. That email is the start of the owned relationship.

Lead with service, not a sales pitch. A QR code to a setup guide, a recipe library, a fit finder or a maintenance schedule is useful, compliant, and it lands the customer on your domain in a helpful frame of mind. Encouraging future orders and promoting your other products is allowed on the insert. Directing them off-platform to buy is not. Build the former.

Own the branded search that the marketplace creates for you. A shopper who discovers you on Amazon often searches your brand name next. That branded search is some of the most valuable traffic you have, and it is worth protecting rather than assuming it is free. [Whether branded search is incremental](/blog/is-branded-search-incremental/) is a question to test, not to guess, but the demand the marketplace generates should land on a DTC experience ready to convert the second order, with a [delivery promise the shopper can trust](/blog/shoppers-need-a-date-they-can-trust/) and the reasons to buy from you spelled out.

Make the reorder effortless. Subscription, auto-replenishment, saved payment and one-tap reorder are things a marketplace copies but your owned store can do better and brand around. The entire point of winning the customer is that the second purchase should take less friction than the first, not more.

## What this looks like for consumables

Supplements, coffee, skincare, pet food, anything a customer finishes and buys again is the easiest version of this strategy, because the second order is not a maybe. It is on a clock. The work is to make the capture genuinely useful and the reorder genuinely better, both without a word that breaks the marketplace's rules.

Start with the capture. For a supplement, the strongest on-ramp is a certificate of analysis lookup: scan the bottle to see this batch's third-party lab results. It is the exact trust signal the category is judged on, it is pure service, and it collects an email for a real reason. A dosing and timing guide, a protocol quiz, or a refill-timing tool that turns a daily serving into a reorder date do the same job. For food and beverage it is recipes, pairing guides, a brewing guide, a roast-date or freshness lookup. For skincare it is a routine builder or a layering tutorial. For pet and baby it is a feeding calculator or a stage profile. None of these say buy here. Every one of them is a reason a real customer wants to be on your site.

Then make the reorder something the marketplace cannot match. Time the subscription to the customer's actual dose instead of a generic monthly ship. Sell the stack or the protocol as a bundle you never list on the marketplace. Offer the pouch refill, the economy size, the members-only formulation, the concentrate that makes no sense to ship as a full bottle of water. For pet and baby, auto-ship sized to the animal or the stage is the most dependable repeat revenue in retail. The hero product earns the customer on the marketplace. The refills, timed and bundled and a little better than anyone else makes them, are why the next order is yours.

Hold one line through all of it. The insert and the page it points to have to be real service. A lab-results page or a recipe hub with an email capture is fine. A page that says reorder cheaper here is diverting the customer off the platform, and that is the rule you cannot break.

## MAP protects the path, it does not start a price war

Minimum Advertised Price is the quiet piece that keeps this strategy from collapsing. MAP is a policy that sets the lowest price a reseller may advertise your product at. Without it, a marketplace seller or a discounter undercuts your DTC price, trains the customer to buy wherever is cheapest, and erases any reason to come to your site.

Used well, MAP does the opposite of a race to the bottom. It holds the advertised price steady across every channel so your own store never looks like the expensive option. That frees you to compete on everything price cannot touch: the warranty, the subscription, the loyalty perks, the bundles, the exclusive SKUs, the faster reorder. You are not pulling the customer to your site with a lower number, which MAP would prohibit anyway. You are pulling them with a better deal that happens to protect your margin. Enforce MAP consistently, or the discounters make the decision for you.

## The number that tells you it is working

Stop reading marketplace revenue and DTC revenue as two separate scoreboards. The number that matters is the migration rate: of the customers who first bought you on a marketplace, what share make their next purchase on your own site. That single figure tells you whether the bridge is real or whether you are renting customers forever and calling it a channel strategy.

Pair it with a blended view of acquisition cost. Amortize the marketplace fee across the full lifetime, not the first order, and compare it to what a first DTC purchase would have cost in ad spend. When you count the second and third orders at full margin, a first sale that looked unprofitable on the marketplace is often the cheapest customer you acquired all year. The cost of a repeat order that [each kind of churn](/blog/four-kinds-of-churn/) you prevent protects belongs in that same math, because keeping the customer is what makes the acquisition pay.

So before the next debate about whether to protect marketplace sales or push harder on DTC, answer one question: what share of your marketplace buyers has ever bought from you directly, and do you have a single, policy-safe path built to move them? If the answer is "we do not track that" and "not really," the strategy is not marketplace versus DTC. It is the bridge you have not built yet.

The two budgets growing fastest in 2026 are also the two with the least independent proof. The IAB's September update puts US commerce and retail media growth at [13.6% this year](https://www.iab.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/IAB_2H_2026_Ad_Spend_Outlook_Study.pdf), on pace to grow 10% faster than the overall ad market, and CTV growth at about 15.6%. Both are outrunning a market already growing 12.3%.

And yet only [15% of retail media advertisers](https://skai.io/blog/the-2026-state-of-retail-media-measurement-and-incrementality/) say they are very or extremely effective at measuring it, and three in four call incrementality their biggest measurement problem. The money is moving faster than the proof. That gap is the whole story, and it has a structural cause.

## Where the money is going

Retail media is now a top-tier budget line. Amazon's advertising services revenue alone was [$19.8 billion in the second quarter of 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001018724/000101872426000024/amzn-20260630xex991.htm), up from $15.7 billion a year earlier. CTV is growing faster still, at about 15.6% for the year. A useful marker of how mainstream CTV buying has become: AdImpact projects 2026 midterm political ad spend at a record [$11.6 billion, with $2.6 billion going to CTV](https://ppc.land/midterm-ad-spending-overtakes-the-2024-presidential-race/). When political buyers, who care about nothing but reaching the right person, put that much into CTV, the channel has arrived. The question is whether the measurement arrived with it.

It mostly did not, and the reason is that these budgets grew up inside the sellers. A retail media network is a retailer first and a media company second, so its measurement was built to sell more ads, not to answer whether the ads worked. CTV inherited the opposite problem: it came from television, where nobody expected a click, so the proof was always going to be modeled rather than counted. Fast growth plus seller-built measurement is how you get the two largest new line items in the plan resting on the least independent evidence.

## The seller is also the scorekeeper

Here is the structural problem with retail media. A retail media network sells you the ad, serves it on its own shelf to a shopper who is already in the aisle, and then reports the sale that ad "drove." The party making money from the ad is the party grading it. That is [grading its own homework](/blog/platform-roas-is-grading-its-own-homework/) at the exact point of purchase.

The buy side knows. The IAB's January outlook said that in commerce media, "fragmentation, measurement inconsistency, and questions of incrementality continue to persist." NIQ went further, concluding that [almost no brand has the foundation required](https://nielseniq.com/global/en/insights/analysis/2026/retail-media-incrementality-measurement/) for accurate incrementality measurement, even as 67% of CMOs plan to increase retail media investment. The ANA found [55% of advertisers](https://www.ana.net/content/show/id/pr-2024-07-rmn) name a lack of standardization across platforms as the greatest challenge. And there is a market signal worth reading too: at Albertsons Media Collective, the SVP [left after just over a year](https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/brian-monahan-exits-albertsons-media-collective-amid-leadership-shake-up/) and the unit now reports to the CMO, with an agency executive saying their sense was that the retail media side had not grown at the pace hoped. When a network's own momentum wobbles, buyers should ask what the proof behind the growth actually looked like.

The uncomfortable part is that the retailer is not wrong about the sales it reports. Those orders happened. The problem is attribution, not arithmetic. An ad served to a shopper already walking down the aisle toward your product will be followed by a purchase, and the network will record that purchase as driven by the ad, with no way inside the network's own data to know whether the shopper would have bought anyway. That is not a measurement bug to be patched. It is the structural position of a seller grading its own ads, and it does not improve with a nicer dashboard.

## The eBay lesson applies to the aisle

The cleanest experimental evidence we have on ads shown to people who were going to buy anyway is not from retail media. It is from paid search, which is why it is the closest analogue. In large field experiments, eBay found that [brand-keyword ads had no measurable short-term benefit](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20171), and that paid search returns measured experimentally were a fraction of the non-experimental estimates the dashboards produced. A sponsored placement for your own brand on a retailer's search results page is the same question in a different building: you are often paying to be shown to a shopper who already typed your name, the same way [branded search can bill you for demand you already had](/blog/is-branded-search-incremental/).

The retailer will tell you those placements earn a high return, and they are telling the truth as their data sees it. Of course the return looks high. You are paying to be shown to people who already decided, and then counting their decision as yours.

## CTV's version of the problem

CTV has a softer version of the same disease. It is upper-funnel, so last-click reporting genuinely undervalues it, and that gap gets filled with vendor studies that would not survive review. One CTV report claimed a [22.3% lift in paid search conversions](https://ppc.land/ctv-lifts-paid-search-conversions-22-3-lifesight-report-finds/) while giving no sample size, no time period, no vertical composition and no statistical method, and every company on its cover sells CTV. Because last click undercounts it, CTV has to be judged as [demand generation](/blog/demand-generation-vs-demand-capture/), on a controlled read rather than an attributed one. To be fair, when it is tested properly it can look strong: Haus reported that CTV had the [largest median lift of any channel](https://www.haus.io/blog/the-cyber-week-incrementality-report-how-ctv-youtube-and-paid-social-drive-roi) in its 2025 Cyber Week incrementality tests, 344% in the post-treatment window, though that is a vendor report and the CTV test count was not given.

So CTV is the channel most likely to be both undervalued by the tools most teams use and oversold by the studies most vendors produce. Both can be true at once, which is exactly why it needs a real test rather than either dashboard. The honest position on CTV is not that it works or that it does not. It is that you cannot know until you hold it out, and most teams have never once held it out.

## What proof to ask for

The burden of proof has to move from the seller's dashboard to a test the buyer controls. Four things to require.

First, new-to-brand share, with the definition and window in writing, because the definition does the heavy lifting: Walmart Connect reported that [46% of ad-attributed revenue](https://www.walmartconnect.com/goals/acquire-new-customers) in fiscal 2025 came from new-to-brand customers, using a 14-day window and no prior purchase in 12 months. Change the window and the number moves. Second, a holdout the buyer controls, by geo, store cluster or audience, which is the only read that survives [a proper design](/blog/how-to-run-a-geo-holdout-test/). Third, incremental return, not attributed return, the [number a CFO actually trusts](/blog/incremental-roas-the-number-a-cfo-trusts/). Fourth, a spend ceiling per network until a test clears it, because every channel hits [the point where the next dollar stops working](/blog/the-saturation-point/).

I have run this the hard way. At a large retailer I ran growth for, CTV and programmatic sat inside a $36M annual paid media budget, and every channel was read three ways: the platform's report, a post-purchase survey, and incrementality tests. And at a subscription wellness brand, I cut DSP spend that could not show it was adding customers. The read decided the budget, not the pitch.

## Be fair: some of it works

None of this means the channels fail. Instacart says its brand partners see a [25% average sales lift](https://company.instacart.com/instacart-ads/growth-incremental-sales-and-scale) from its own internal tests over four quarters, Walmart Connect reports meaningful new-to-brand share, and Haus's tested CTV lift was large. Some retail media and CTV spend is genuinely incremental. The claim is narrower and harder to dodge: the evidence most buyers accept today comes from the seller, and that is the one source that cannot settle the question. Ask for the holdout. If the channel works, the test will say so, and then you can scale it on a number you actually trust instead of one the seller handed you.

For the retail media or CTV dollars in your plan, what proof did you accept, and who produced it?

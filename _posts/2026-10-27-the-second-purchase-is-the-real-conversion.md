Most growth teams stop counting at the first order. That is where the channel gets its credit, where the offer gets judged, and where CAC gets calculated. It is also the cheapest order to buy. Every acquisition lever ever invented is aimed at producing exactly one first order.

The order that tells you whether you acquired a customer or a transaction is the second one. Smile.io's network data puts the chance a first-time buyer comes back at [27%, rising to 49% after a second purchase and 62% after a third](https://blog.smile.io/repeat-customers-profitable/). The jump from the first order to the second is the steepest climb in the whole relationship. Clear it and the odds roughly double. That is the real conversion.

## The first order is the easiest one to buy

A discount produces a first order. Retargeting produces a first order. A platform's own attribution is built to claim a first order. So a scorecard that stops at the first order flatters every one of those levers at once, and it flatters them most when costs are high and teams are reaching hardest. Foxwell Digital and Northbeam found [76% of more than 600 marketing leaders](https://foxwelldigital.com/blog/navigating-q4-2026-insights-from-the-foxwell-founders-amp-northbeam-marketing-survey) named rising CPMs and auction costs their top Q4 2026 concern, with 38% aiming for non-discount holiday messaging. When the first order costs more to buy, the temptation to buy it anyway and call it growth gets stronger. The fix is to insist that spend show [incremental return, not attributed return](/blog/incremental-roas-the-number-a-cfo-trusts/).

The second order resists that gaming. You cannot retarget someone into coming back a second time the way you can nudge them into a first purchase with a well-placed ad. The second order has to be earned by the product, the experience and the fit between what you sold and who you sold it to. That is exactly why it is the better scorecard: it measures the thing the first order cannot fake, which is whether you acquired someone who actually wanted what you sell.

## The second order shows up fast

The useful thing about second-order rate is that you do not have to wait a year for it. Optimove's data on more than three million fashion and beauty customers found [39% of first-time customers made a second purchase within a month and 62.5% within three](https://www.optimove.com/blog/turning-one-timers-into-loyal-customers), and that one-time customers' order value ran 23% below repeat buyers'. Most of the second orders that are ever going to happen happen inside a quarter. That makes second-order rate a leading indicator you can read now, unlike lifetime value, which asks you to wait until the answer no longer helps.

The benchmarks vary by source, which is why you anchor on your own. Bluecore found an [average repeat purchase rate of 16.5%](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2024/04/16/2863832/0/en/Retailers-With-the-Highest-Identification-Rates-See-53-Higher-Repeat-Purchase-Rates-Finds-New-Report-from-Bluecore.html) across more than 100 retailers in 2023, with health and beauty at 21.5% and apparel at 20.2%, and separately that shoppers were [15.07% more likely to buy again](https://www.bluecore.com/press/bluecore-benchmark-report-reveals-retailers-revenue-from-existing-shoppers/) after a first purchase and 25.59% after a second. Different data sets, different definitions. Do not average them. Use them to set your own target and read it as a [cohort curve](/blog/cohort-retention-analysis/), not a single blended rate.

Reading it as a curve matters because the average hides the mechanism. Two channels can post the same blended repeat rate while one brings in customers who buy again in three weeks and another brings in customers who trickle back over a year, if at all. The first channel is building a business; the second is renting one. A blended number calls them equal. The cohort curve, cut by acquisition source, shows you which is which while you still have time to move the budget.

## Discounts: the research disagrees, so measure it

The question every team asks is whether a launch discount poisons the customer it brings in. The honest answer is that the research disagrees. Michael Lewis, in the Journal of Marketing Research, found that a [35% acquisition discount](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.43.2.195) produced customers with about half the long-term value of those acquired without one, and that discount depth was negatively related to repeat buying. But Anderson and Simester, in catalog field experiments, found the opposite for new buyers: [deeper discounts increased future purchases by first-time customers](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/anderson_e/htm/personalpage_files/Papers/Long_Run_Effects_of_Promotion_Depth_on_New_versus_Established_Customers.pdf), while reducing them among established ones.

Both are good studies. They disagree because the effect depends on the business, the category and who is already in the file. The takeaway is not "never discount." It is that a discount is a bet on the next order, and you cannot assume which way it pays. Discount-led acquisition can bring in [cohorts that get worse over time](/blog/cohorts-getting-worse-acquisition/), or it can seed a habit, and only your own second-order data by offer tells you which. If your category rewards confidence over markdowns, the [luxury playbook for a $99 dress](/blog/luxury-playbook-for-the-99-dollar-dress/) is the other side of the same bet.

Run the two side by side: customers acquired at full price, and customers acquired on the launch discount, each tracked to their second order. The gap between those two curves is the real cost of the promotion, and it is usually paid later than the quarter that booked the sale. A discount that looks free on the first order is often financed out of the second one that never comes.

## Fund acquisition on the second order

Four moves turn this into a budgeting discipline.

First, report second-order rate at 30, 60 and 90 days by acquisition channel and by first-order offer. That one table reranks your channels. Second, set CAC targets against contribution margin from the first two orders, not one, so the [payback math](/blog/cohort-payback-period/) reflects the customer rather than the transaction. Third, treat a channel with high first-order volume and a low second-order rate as a cost, not a win, until a holdout says otherwise. Fourth, remember that the welcome and onboarding flow helps at the margin but is not the main lever; who you acquired matters more than what you email them, and the [retention program](/lifecycle/) is for the customers earlier work actually [earned the right to keep](/blog/intervene-before-the-cancel/).

I have watched the second order carry an entire model. At a subscription wellness brand I ran as a fractional head of growth, orders per customer went from 2.0 to 3.5, retention rose 33% and [churn](/blog/four-kinds-of-churn/) fell 62%. The second order was the whole business, and improving the [unit math](/blog/the-unit-math-of-subscriptions/) behind it did more than any acquisition push could have.

The order of operations is the lesson. We did not fix retention by emailing harder. We changed who came in the door, then made the first experience good enough that the second order did not need a coupon to happen.

## When there is no second order

Not every business gets a second order, and pretending otherwise is its own mistake. Repeat purchase rates are [lower for high-value goods](https://klaviyo.com/glossary/what-is-a-good-repeat-purchase-rate) like tech or luxury items, and in one-purchase categories, the next-order test simply does not apply.

I have run growth inside exactly that constraint. At a large retailer I ran growth for, the purchase was a once-in-a-lifetime dress. Repeat was structurally thin: loyalty members bought 1.5 times in a year and non-members 1.2. So the next order was not the lever. Units per transaction was, along with first-order payback, because you cannot retention your way out of a bad CAC. In a one-purchase category the discipline shifts to the first transaction: what it pays back, and what else you can honestly sell inside it.

If you ranked your acquisition channels by second-order rate instead of first-order volume, would the top one stay on top?

Watch one customer move through a week. She sees your connected TV spot on Sunday, skips past a YouTube pre-roll on Monday, asks an AI assistant for a recommendation on Tuesday, taps an Instagram ad on Wednesday, searches your name on Google on Thursday, and checks out on your site on Friday. Six platforms, six different jobs, and only the last screen was ever going to post as the conversion. Grade all six on that screen and you will defund the five that set it up. This is the named-platform companion to the stage map in [every paid channel is good at one job](/blog/every-paid-channel-has-one-job/): the same argument, applied to the specific platforms a [paid media plan](/paid-media/) actually buys.

## One number, six jobs

The most expensive habit in paid media is a single return-on-ad-spend target pointed at every platform at once. The platform sitting closest to the purchase always wins that comparison, because it gets the credit for a decision the earlier platforms set in motion. So connected TV looks wasteful and gets cut, brand search looks like a hero and gets more budget, and total revenue does not move, because you defunded the platform starting journeys and overfunded the one that was only there at the finish. That is the platform's own report doing exactly what it is built to do, which is [grade its own homework](/blog/platform-roas-is-grading-its-own-homework/) and flatter the last click.

## Connected TV and YouTube: create the demand

The job at the top is to create demand among people who are not looking for you yet, and connected TV and YouTube are built for it. They reach an audience before intent and start the journey other platforms later finish. That job is real, and it is the one most likely to be killed by the wrong metric, because a last-click number cannot see demand a platform created, only demand it happened to touch last.

Connected TV also carries [the weakest proof of any channel](/blog/ctv-and-retail-media-weakest-proof/) when you accept the seller's own lift study as the answer. The honest read is a controlled one: a [geo holdout](/blog/how-to-run-a-geo-holdout-test/) that withholds the spend from a comparable set of markets and measures the gap. Judge these platforms by a capture metric and you will conclude they do not work, right up until you turn them off and watch capture dry up.

## Instagram and paid social: demand creation the dashboard calls capture

Instagram and the rest of paid social mostly do the top-of-funnel job too, reaching people before they were searching. The trap is that social platforms report a conversion they can associate with the tap, which dresses a demand-creation platform in a demand-capture number. Much of that reported return is demand you already had, counted again. Worse, the platforms keep changing what they count, so a reported number can move while nothing in the market does, which is why every team needs to track [when the ruler moves](/blog/when-the-ruler-moves/) before reading a jump or a drop as performance.

## The AI assistant: the recommendation you cannot bid on

A new step now sits between awareness and purchase: the AI assistant. More buyers move from a need to an assistant that researches and recommends, and only then to a store. That recommendation layer is in the middle of the journey, and you cannot buy your way into it the way you buy a search placement. The assistant recommends from your owned footprint and the sources it trusts, not from your ad account. Paid media cannot place the recommendation, but upper-funnel spend and the content it funds build the presence these engines draw on, which is the whole question of [whether your brand is in the AI answer at all](/blog/why-your-brand-is-not-in-the-ai-answer/).

## Google: two platforms wearing one name

Google is where the single-number habit does the most damage, because it is really two platforms with one login. Non-brand search and shopping capture demand that already exists, meeting people at the moment of intent, and here a click-based metric is close to fair, since the click really is near the decision. The discipline for capture is sizing: spend to the real volume of unmet intent, not past the point where you are paying to be present for buyers who would have found you anyway.

Brand search is the other platform, and it harvests demand rather than creating it. It reaches people who already chose you and collects credit for conversions that were going to happen regardless. It is worth running and worth far less than its dashboard claims, and the only way to know how much is real is to withhold it from a random group and measure.

## The honest read needs permission, not just math

Every controlled read above has the same precondition, and it is organizational, not statistical. To measure a create or harvest platform you have to turn its spend off for a defined window, and the hard part is rarely the test design. It is [getting permission to go dark long enough to read the result](/blog/permission-not-statistics/) when the dashboard is still reporting that the spend is working. The teams that win this argument are the ones who agreed, before the test, that they wanted the real number.

## The website checkout: the only number that is the business

The last screen is the only one that is not a platform's claim about itself. The checkout is your own booked revenue, the counterfactual none of the ad accounts can see. Every platform upstream is making a case for credit; the checkout is the fact the cases are competing to explain. Which is exactly why it is the wrong scoreboard for the five platforms that preceded it: it is the result they all contributed to, not the job any one of them was hired to do.

## The whole map on one page

| Platform | The one job | Honest metric | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Connected TV, YouTube | Create demand | Incrementality / lift | Judged on last-click, cut while working |
| Instagram, paid social | Create demand | Lift, not platform-reported conversions | Reported as capture, double-counted |
| AI assistant | Earn the recommendation | Presence and citation in answers | Assumed unbuyable, left unfunded |
| Google non-brand search, shopping | Capture demand | Efficiency at the margin | Overspent past real unmet demand |
| Google brand search | Harvest demand | Incremental lift via holdout | Sized off inflated last-click returns |
| Website checkout | Close and record | Booked contribution margin | Used to grade the five platforms before it |

Read the metric column and the argument is the whole table: most of these jobs are invisible or distorted under last-click, which is why one blended ROAS number mismanages a plan built from six different platforms. The platforms are not the problem. The scoreboard is. So before the next budget cycle grades every platform on the same screen, name the job each one is actually being paid to do, and ask whether you are measuring it on that job or on the one that happens last.

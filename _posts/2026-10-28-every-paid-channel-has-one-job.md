Each paid channel does one job well. Connected TV, YouTube, and paid social prospecting create demand. Paid search and shopping capture it. Retargeting and brand search harvest it. Lifecycle keeps it. Most waste comes from judging a demand-creation channel by a demand-capture metric, so you cut the channel that was working and credit the one that was only there at the end.

## The expensive mistake

The most common failure in paid media is not a bad channel. It is one return-on-ad-spend number graded across every stage of the journey at once. When a single ROAS target judges the channel that creates demand and the channel that merely closes it, the math is rigged before anyone looks at it. The channel sitting closest to the purchase will always post the highest number, because it gets the credit for a decision that other spend set in motion.

So the top-funnel channel looks inefficient and gets cut, and the bottom-funnel channel looks like a hero and gets more budget, and total revenue does not move, because you defunded the thing that was actually starting journeys and overfunded the thing that was only there to witness the finish. This is the platform's own report doing what it is built to do, which is [grade its own homework](/blog/platform-roas-is-grading-its-own-homework/) and flatter the last click. The fix is not a better ROAS target. It is to stop asking one metric to judge four different jobs.

## Demand creation: the top of the funnel

The job at the top is to create demand that did not exist yet, among people who are not searching for you. Connected TV, YouTube, podcasts, and paid social prospecting are the channels built for it. They reach an audience before intent, plant the brand, and start the journey that other channels later finish. That is a real and valuable job, and it is the one most likely to be killed by the wrong metric.

The honest measure for these channels is incrementality, not last-click return. You read them with a holdout or a lift test that compares a treated group against a controlled one and measures the gap, because that is the only way to see demand the channel actually created rather than demand it happened to touch. This is the discipline of running [one demand engine measured one way](/blog/one-demand-engine-measured-one-way/), and it is why the distinction between [demand generation and demand capture](/blog/demand-generation-vs-demand-capture/) is the most important line in the plan. Judge demand creation by a capture metric and you will always conclude it does not work, right up until you turn it off and watch capture dry up.

## The new middle: the AI-answer layer

Here is what has changed, and it is the part most channel plans have not caught up to. A new step has appeared between awareness and purchase: the AI assistant. More buyers now move from "I have a need" to a shopping assistant that researches and recommends, and only then to a purchase. That recommendation layer sits squarely in the middle of the funnel, and you cannot buy your way into it the way you buy a search placement.

The assistant recommends from your owned footprint and from the sources it trusts, not from your ad account. So paid media cannot place the recommendation, but it is not powerless either. Upper-funnel spend and the content it funds build the brand presence and the corroboration these engines draw on, which means your awareness budget now has a second job: feeding a surface it cannot bid on. This is the next turn of the shift where [AI search forces paid back upstream](/blog/ai-search-is-forcing-paid-back-upstream/), toward building demand and authority rather than only capturing clicks. The brands that will win the middle are the ones whose paid and content investment makes them the name the assistant already trusts, which is a question of [whether you are in the AI answer at all](/blog/why-your-brand-is-not-in-the-ai-answer/).

## Demand capture: meeting real intent

Lower down, the job flips from creating demand to capturing it. Non-brand paid search and shopping are the channels for it, because they meet people at the exact moment they are looking for what you sell. This is where intent already exists and your job is to be present and competitive when it surfaces.

These channels deserve a capture metric, and here last-click is closer to fair, because the click really is near the decision. The discipline is sizing: capture spend should scale with the real volume of unmet intent, not past the point where you are simply paying to be present for demand that would have found you anyway. Capture is the easiest stage to measure and the easiest to overspend, because the numbers look good right up to the point where the next dollar is buying a customer who was already walking through the door.

## Harvest: brand search, retargeting, and the mirror

At the bottom sits the harvest: brand search and retargeting. These are the channels most likely to report spectacular returns and least likely to be creating them. The reason is structural. Both reach people who have already chosen you, so they collect credit for conversions that were going to happen with or without the ad.

The evidence here is not opinion. In a large field experiment, eBay turned off brand-keyword search ads and found [no measurable short-term benefit](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20171), even though attribution had been crediting those ads with real revenue, which is the same reason [branded search so often looks incremental when it is not](/blog/is-branded-search-incremental/). More broadly, the returns to advertising are so noisy relative to total sales that most observational methods cannot measure them reliably, as Lewis and Rao showed in [work published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics](https://ideas.repec.org/r/oup/qjecon/v130y2015i4p1941-1973.html). Retargeting is the mirror version of the same trap: a large reflected figure that is mostly the demand already in motion, which is the whole point of separating [prospecting from retargeting](/blog/prospecting-vs-retargeting/). The only way to know how much of the harvest is real is to withhold it from a random group and measure, with a [geo holdout](/blog/how-to-run-a-geo-holdout-test/) or the [right incrementality test for the channel](/blog/which-incrementality-test-to-run/). Harvest channels are worth running. They are just worth far less than their dashboards claim, and sizing them off the reported number is how budgets quietly fill with spend that changes nothing.

## Retention: the cheapest incremental dollar

One step is cheaper than all of them and usually underfunded: keeping the customer you already paid to acquire. Post-purchase paid and lifecycle work operate on people who have already bought, where the cost to move the next purchase is a fraction of the cost to create the first. The job here is not acquisition dressed up as retention. It is extending value from customers already in the base, and measured properly, with a holdout against the people who would have come back anyway, it is often the highest-return work in the plan. It is also the lane most brands treat as an afterthought precisely because last-click does not make it look like a hero.

## The whole map on one page

Put it together and the plan stops being a pile of channels fighting over one ROAS target and becomes a map, where each channel has a job, a step, an honest metric, and a failure mode you can name in advance.

| Channel | The one job | Step it moves | Honest metric | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CTV, YouTube, podcast, social prospecting | Create demand | Awareness | Incrementality / lift | Judged on last-click ROAS, cut while working |
| Content + brand presence | Feed the AI recommendation | The new middle | Presence and citation share in AI answers | Assumed unbuyable, so left unfunded |
| Non-brand paid search, shopping | Capture demand | Intent | Efficiency at the margin | Overspent past real unmet demand |
| Brand search, retargeting | Harvest demand | Decision | Incremental lift via holdout | Sized off inflated last-click returns |
| Post-purchase paid, lifecycle | Keep the customer | Retention | Incremental repeat vs holdout | Underfunded because last-click hides it |

Read down the metric column and the pattern is the whole argument: three of the five jobs are invisible or distorted under last-click, which is exactly why one blended ROAS number mismanages the plan. The channels are not the problem. The scoreboard is. So before the next budget cycle grades every channel on the same number, which job is each channel in your plan actually being paid to do, and are you measuring it on that job or on the one that happens last?

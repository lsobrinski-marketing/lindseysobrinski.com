I came up in performance marketing. Spending on awareness is not the sentence I expected to write. Here is the number that changed my mind. Out of every 100 shopping chats with an AI, 47 ended in a Google search within 30 minutes, and only 2 went straight to a store. The AI did the research. Paid search caught the click. Your dashboard is giving paid search the credit, and the work that actually sold the customer has no line on the report.

## The shopper decides in AI, then Googles to buy

New panel data from Searchable puts a number on a shift most teams can already feel. Across shopping conversations with an AI, 47 of every 100 ended with a Google search inside 30 minutes. Two went straight to a retailer like Amazon or Walmart. The rest went to another website, to a store or social, or nowhere at all for half an hour. People ask the AI what to buy, then they open Google to act on the answer.

The checkout numbers run the same direction. Shoppers who Googled right after the chat reached a checkout page 28% of the time within a day. Shoppers who sat idle after the chat reached one 12% of the time. The Google search is not the decision. It is the receipt for a decision made somewhere you cannot see.

One caveat before anyone puts this in a deck. This is one vendor's opt-in US and UK panel, a 10% sample, run over the summer, and Searchable sells into exactly this space, so the exact 47 is directional, not gospel. The mechanism underneath it is the durable part, and it matches what practitioners are watching happen in their own funnels. Treat the number as a signal and the pattern as the point.

The pattern is the point. Discovery and consideration moved off your site and into a chat window. That is the same move covered in [why your customers are shopping like they are choosing a surgeon](/blog/shopping-like-choosing-a-surgeon/): the shopper lets an AI do the research and hands back a shortlist, then buys from that. By the time a paid search ad catches them, the choice is mostly made.

## Why your paid search looks like a genius right now

Here is the mechanical reason paid search is posting strong numbers. The shopper arrives pre-sold. They already asked the AI, already got a shortlist, already decided to look at you. Then they type your category or your name into Google, click the ad sitting at the top, and convert. Last-click attribution sees a paid search click followed by a purchase and does the obvious thing. It credits the ad.

But the ad did not create that demand. It intercepted it. The persuasion happened in the AI conversation, and that conversation gets zero credit, because it leaves no click in your analytics. The channel closest to the purchase takes the trophy for a decision other work set in motion. The site has made the case before that [platform ROAS is grading its own homework](/blog/platform-roas-is-grading-its-own-homework/). The AI research layer makes it worse, because now the homework was done by a system that does not even show up in your reports.

This is not a new kind of error. It is the oldest one in the book, running on a new journey. [Every paid channel is good at one job](/blog/every-paid-channel-has-one-job/), and the harvest channels at the bottom always look the best, because they stand closest to the money. What changed is where the demand gets born. It used to be seeded across your own marketing. Now a large share of it is seeded in a chat you do not own and cannot measure.

## The part no one is saying: the work moved back to awareness

So here is what I am not hearing anyone say, and I am a performance person saying it. The response to this shift is not to go bid harder inside ChatGPT. It is to spend on awareness again.

Walk the journey backward. The sale happened at checkout. The checkout followed a Google search. The Google search followed an AI shortlist. And the AI put you on that shortlist because it had reason to, and the shopper accepted it because they already knew your name. Two things decide whether you make the cut: whether the engine trusts you enough to name you, and whether the human recognizes you enough to pick you. Both are built long before the chat. Both are awareness.

The AI trust half is its own discipline. Getting named in the answer is a credibility test, not a ranking test, and what moves it is covered in [why your brand is not in the AI answer](/blog/why-your-brand-is-not-in-the-ai-answer/) and in [what it takes to get named in AI answers](/blog/what-it-takes-to-get-named-in-ai-answers/). The human half is plain old brand. When an AI hands a shopper two or three options, recognition breaks the tie. The shopper clicks the name they already trust and reads the unfamiliar one with suspicion. You cannot buy that recognition at the moment of the search. You build it upstream, or you discount to cover for not having it.

That is the whole case. The decision moved upstream, so the money has to move upstream to meet it. This is the next mile of the argument the site made when [AI search started forcing paid back upstream](/blog/ai-search-is-forcing-paid-back-upstream/). Then it was about demand capture getting scarce and expensive. Now it is sharper. The single highest-leverage spend is the one that makes you the brand the AI names and the shopper already wanted, and that spend is awareness.

## Why no one funds it: the dashboard cannot see the top of the funnel

If awareness is the lever, why is every budget still pointed at the bottom of the funnel? Because the scoreboard was built to be blind to the top.

Awareness creates demand that shows up later, somewhere else, as a direct visit, a branded search, or an organic click. None of those carry a tag that says the awareness campaign sent them. Last-click attribution hands that conversion to whatever stood closest to it, which is almost always a capture channel. So awareness looks like money with nothing to show for it, and the capture channel looks like a hero. Run that logic through one budget review and you defund the thing that was starting journeys and overfund the thing that was only there to witness the finish. Revenue does not move. You just rearranged the credit.

Google itself has been backing away from last-click for two years. It removed the rules-based models, first-click, linear, time-decay, and position-based, from Ads and Analytics, made data-driven attribution the default, and in 2025 its own retail-media lead told a room of advertisers that the industry needs to move away from last-click and 24-hour sales. Here is the catch almost no one names. Data-driven attribution, the upgrade Google is selling you, still only learns from clicks it can see. The AI conversation is not a click. So the smarter model cannot see the thing that moved the shopper either. The problem was never which clicks you weight. It is that the decisive moment now leaves no click at all.

The proof that this is real and not a thought experiment is on the record. When eBay turned off its brand-keyword search ads, the measurable short-term lift was zero, even though attribution had been crediting those ads with real revenue. The ads were harvesting demand that already existed. Your paid search, catching shoppers who were sold in an AI chat, is running the same play. The report will call it a top performer right up until the day you turn it off and nothing happens.

This is why the person making the awareness case almost has to be a performance marketer. From a brand team it sounds like budget defense. From someone who came up optimizing last-click and has every reason to defend it, it is a confession: the number I was trained to trust is now crediting a channel for work it did not do.

## How to spend on awareness without losing the next budget review

Moving money upstream and keeping the old scoreboard is how awareness budgets die. The first efficiency review claws them right back, because on a last-click report they always look like the weakest line. So you change what you move the money on, not just where.

Measure awareness with incrementality, not attribution. Hold out a set of matched markets, run your awareness spend everywhere else, and compare the lift in total sales, branded search, and direct traffic. The gap is what the awareness actually created, credited to no channel and claimed by none. That is a [geo holdout](/blog/how-to-run-a-geo-holdout-test/), and it is the one test that can see demand creation at all. If you only run one experiment this quarter, [this is how to pick which incrementality test to run](/blog/which-incrementality-test-to-run/).

Then make the ask small enough to approve. A few low-volume markets, a named amount at risk, a fixed window, a readout date. You are not asking the CFO to believe in brand. You are asking to prove whether a channel is incremental, with a bounded test and a date on the calendar. That reframes awareness from an act of faith into a measured bet, and a measured bet survives the review that a faith-based line never will.

The uncomfortable part is that the better your last-click paid search number looks right now, the more demand is being created upstream that you are not paying to create. That is not a reason to feel good about the paid search number. It is the tell that something else is doing your awareness for you, and that you are one algorithm change away from finding out what it was worth. So here is the question for your next plan. If your paid search is converting because the customer already decided in an AI chat, who is doing the work of getting you into that chat, and is anyone funding it on purpose?

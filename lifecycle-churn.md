faq:
  - q: What causes customer churn?
    a: >-
      Four distinct things, and treating them as one is why most churn work
      fails. Involuntary churn is a billing failure: expired cards, declined
      charges, fraud holds, address problems. Never-activated churn is a
      customer who bought but never reached the value they bought for.
      Completed churn is a customer who got exactly what they came for and has
      no remaining need. Dissatisfied churn is a customer who got value and
      decided it was not worth the price. Each has a different owner, a
      different fix, and a different economic value, so the first job is always
      to size them separately.
  - q: How do you calculate churn correctly?
    a: >-
      By cohort, not in aggregate. A monthly churn rate divides everyone who
      left by everyone who was there, which blends a first-month customer with
      a third-year customer and averages away the shape you need to see.
      Retention curves grouped by acquisition cohort show when customers leave,
      which is the part you can act on. If churn concentrates in the first
      sixty days, that is an onboarding problem. If the curve is flat and then
      drops at month twelve, that is a renewal or a pricing problem. The
      aggregate number gives you the same answer either way, which is to say no
      answer.
  - q: What is a good churn rate?
    a: >-
      Less useful a question than it looks, because the honest benchmark is your
      own cohort payback period. A 5% monthly churn rate is fine if the customer
      pays back acquisition cost in month two and catastrophic if payback takes
      nine months. The number that matters is whether the average customer
      generates more contribution margin than they cost to acquire before they
      leave, and whether that gap is widening or narrowing across cohorts.
      Comparing your churn rate to an industry average tells you almost nothing
      about your own economics.
  - q: How much churn is involuntary?
    a: >-
      On most subscription businesses, 20 to 40% of total churn, and on some
      payment-heavy models more. It is consistently the largest single
      recoverable pool in the business and consistently the least worked,
      because it belongs to no one: marketing sees a cancellation, finance sees
      a decline, and the fix lives in dunning logic, card-updater services,
      retry timing, and pre-expiration outreach. Recovering half of it is
      ordinary, and it costs a fraction of what acquiring the replacement
      customer would.
  - q: What are the leading indicators of churn?
    a: >-
      Behavioral, and they show up well before the cancellation. Falling order
      frequency against the customer's own established interval, a skipped
      cycle, a narrowing of category breadth, a drop in engagement with the
      product or the emails they used to open, a support contact that went
      badly, a delivery that arrived late. The useful version measures each
      customer against their own baseline rather than a global average, because
      a customer who orders monthly going quiet for six weeks means something
      that the same six weeks means nothing about for a quarterly buyer.
  - q: Does a win-back campaign actually reduce churn?
    a: >-
      It recovers some customers, which is not the same thing. Win-back operates
      after the decision has been made, so it is the most expensive point in the
      lifecycle to intervene and the one with the lowest ceiling. It is also the
      easiest place to fool yourself: a share of lapsed customers return on
      their own, and a win-back program with no holdout will report every one of
      them as a save. Reducing churn happens upstream, in activation, in
      payment recovery, and in the risk window before the customer has decided.
      Win-back is what you run for the ones you did not catch there.

## Churn is four problems added together

Every churn conversation starts with a single number, and the single number is
the reason the conversation goes nowhere. A percentage of customers left. It
does not say whether they left because a card expired, because they never
figured out what they bought, because they finished what they came to do, or
because the thing was not worth the money. Those are four businesses problems
with four owners, and they are being handed to whoever runs email.

So the first piece of work is not an intervention. It is a decomposition.

**Involuntary.** The payment failed. The card expired, the charge declined, the
billing address went stale, a fraud rule fired. These customers did not decide
anything. They are usually a fifth to two fifths of total churn, they are the
cheapest to recover of any group, and the fix is dunning logic and card updater
coverage rather than a campaign.

**Never activated.** They bought, and then never reached the value they bought
for. The box arrived and stayed unopened, the account was created and never
configured, the second order never happened. This group churns early and looks
like a marketing failure while usually being an onboarding one.

**Completed.** They got exactly what they came for. The project ended, the
child outgrew the size, the course finished. This is not a failure at all, and
treating it as one produces win-back campaigns aimed at people with no remaining
need. The right response is a different offer, or an honest acceptance that this
cohort has a natural life.

**Dissatisfied.** They got the value and decided it was not worth the price. The
product, the service, or the price is the problem, and the only useful thing
marketing can do is report it accurately to the people who can change it.

Size these four before proposing anything. The mix decides the entire program,
and it is different at every business I have looked at.

> A churn rate is a symptom presented as a diagnosis. Nobody would accept "the
> patient has a temperature" as a treatment plan, but that is exactly what a
> monthly churn percentage is.

## Cohorts, because the average hides the shape

A blended churn rate mixes a customer in their first month with one in their
third year and reports the result as a single number. It is arithmetically
correct and analytically useless, because everything you can act on lives in
*when* people leave, not how many.

Group customers by the period they were acquired and follow each group forward.
The curve tells you where the business is actually leaking:

- **A steep early drop** is an activation problem. Customers are not reaching
  value fast enough, and everything you spend downstream is spent on people who
  never got started.
- **A steady, gentle decline** is normal attrition, and the lever is frequency
  and breadth rather than retention messaging.
- **A cliff at a specific tenure** is structural: an annual renewal, the end of
  a promotional price, the point where a consumable would have run out.
- **Cohorts that get progressively worse** mean acquisition quality is falling,
  which is a media problem wearing a retention costume. This one gets
  misdiagnosed constantly, and it is the most expensive mistake on the list.

That last case is worth dwelling on. When retention degrades cohort over cohort
while the retention program has not changed, the program is not what broke. The
customers changed, usually because acquisition scaled into a worse audience, and
no amount of lifecycle work fixes a sourcing problem.

## The first ninety days do most of the work

Across almost every business model, early behavior is disproportionately
predictive of everything after it. The customer who reaches the second order, or
the habit, or whatever your equivalent of first real value is, behaves
differently forever. The customer who does not is largely gone regardless of
what you send them in month seven.

This makes the onboarding window the highest-leverage stretch of the entire
lifecycle, and it is routinely under-resourced relative to win-back, which is
the lowest-leverage stretch. The interval before the second purchase is the most
predictive number most brands do not track, and time-to-second-order is a better
early warning system than any churn model built on the same data.

The practical work here is unglamorous: get the customer to the thing that
delivers value, remove the steps between purchase and that moment, and set the
replenishment or usage rhythm early rather than letting the customer invent one.

## Intervene before the decision, not after it

By the time someone cancels, the decision is old. It was made weeks earlier and
the cancellation is just the paperwork. A churn program that begins at the
cancel screen is intervening at the one point where the customer has already
committed to leaving and where your only remaining tool is a discount.

The risk window is where the money is. That means detecting behavioral drift
against each customer's own baseline, not a global one, and acting while the
customer is still ambivalent. It also means resisting the reflex to lead with a
price cut, because the customer who is retained with a discount at the first
sign of drift has now been taught what to do the next time they get bored.

And it means fixing the involuntary pool first. Payment recovery does not
require anyone to be persuaded of anything. It is operational, it is boring, and
it is nearly always the largest recoverable number sitting in the business.

## Prove it against a holdout, or do not claim it

A retention program without a control group produces a report of customers who
stayed, not evidence that the program kept them. Some share of every at-risk
audience was going to stay anyway, and a program with no holdout will happily
bill you for all of them.

Hold back a percentage of each audience, read performance as the difference
between the two groups, and hold the retention program to the same standard I
hold paid media to. This is what separates a retention program from an expensive
way to be nice to people who already liked you, and it is the only version of
the number that survives contact with a CFO.

## How I work this lane

I decompose the churn first, because the mix determines the program and the mix
is never what the team assumed. Then I work the cohort curves directly rather
than receiving them summarized, because the behavior worth finding is always in
the detail a blended average erases. Then interventions get built in order of
recoverable value, which almost always means involuntary churn and the
activation window before anything that could be described as a campaign.
